The 2026 midterm elections

Court rulings, election rules and results for the Nov. 3, 2026 elections.

  1. Impact 3

    No Kings rallies against President Trump were held Saturday in cities across the U.S., this time urging people to vote early in the Nov. 3 midterms.

    The anti-Trump No Kings network, which has held marches and demonstrations for nearly two years, billed Saturday's hundreds of planned events as nonpartisan. At the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, a band played for what appeared to be a few hundred people holding signs such as "VOTE EARLY" and "HANDS OFF MY VOTE!" In Atlanta, City Councilman Michael Julian Bond urged the crowd to get others to vote before the Nov. 3 general election.

    Why impact 3: The movement behind nearly two years of anti-Trump protests is now aiming at turnout in the midterm elections, about two weeks away.

  2. Impact 3

    Letters with suspicious powder mailed to election offices and senators nationwide are under FBI investigation, three weeks before Election Day.

    The FBI and U.S. Postal Inspection Service said they are investigating a series of suspicious mailings to secretary of state and election offices, some containing an unknown substance, and are working to identify who sent them; Capitol Police are also involved. Mailings led to election-office evacuations this week in Louisiana and Oklahoma, and offices from Minnesota to Idaho to Florida reported letters received or intercepted. Letters to the offices of Sens. Amy Klobuchar, Lisa Blunt Rochester, Alex Padilla and Kirsten Gillibrand were intercepted. Wisconsin said tests found the powder it received was not hazardous. No injuries have been reported.

    Why impact 3: It is a direct threat to the people running the Nov. 3 midterm vote.

    U.S.AP ↗
  3. Impact 3

    California was sued by the Justice Department to stop it from counting mail ballots from about 20,000 people DOJ calls likely noncitizens until their citizenship is checked.

    The suit, filed Oct. 15 in federal court in Los Angeles against the state, Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County Registrar Dean Logan, asks a judge to have those mail ballots set aside and each voter's citizenship confirmed before they are counted in the Nov. 3 election. DOJ says the Department of Homeland Security built the list by checking voter files against federal immigration data. Weber says DOJ has offered no evidence or method behind the list, and Attorney General Rob Bonta said the state will not hold back ballots.

    Why impact 3: Voting is underway, and the case decides whether thousands of mailed ballots are counted in the midterms.

    U.S. District Court for the Central District of CaliforniaU.S. Department of Justice ↗
  4. Impact 3

    The FCC does not have to rule within 48 hours on Democratic candidates' challenge to cheaper campaign ad rates for party committees, the Supreme Court said.

    In an unsigned opinion Oct. 14 in FCC v. Brown (26A476), the Court stayed an Oct. 7 Fourth Circuit order that gave the FCC until Oct. 9 to decide the candidates' application, saying the Communications Act sets no deadline and the appeals court could not force the agency to cut short its process. The FCC's March notice saying parties engaged in coordinated activity get the law's lowest ad rates stays in place; the Court had already put the Fourth Circuit's earlier ruling on hold Sept. 4. Justices Sotomayor and Jackson would have denied the stay.

    Why impact 3: Party committees keep access to the lowest TV ad rates in the final weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms.

    Supreme CourtSupreme Court of the United States (opinion) ↗
  5. Impact 2

    A federal judge extended her order blocking taxpayer-funded TV ads promoting President Trump to Nov. 8 and ordered the government to explain why the ads aired after she halted them.

    U.S. District Judge Jennifer L. Rochon extended her Oct. 11 temporary restraining order and stay by 14 days, finding good cause. She allowed Common Cause and the other plaintiffs limited expedited discovery for a preliminary-injunction motion due Oct. 22 and set a hearing for Nov. 4. She ordered the government to file declarations by Oct. 15 explaining why the challenged ads aired on Oct. 12, given its lawyers' contrary representations.

    Why impact 2: The ads stay off the air through the Nov. 3 midterms, and the court is now probing why its order was not followed.

    U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New YorkU.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York (order) ↗
  6. Impact 3

    The taxpayer-funded TV ads promoting President Trump must be canceled, a federal judge in New York ordered Sunday night, including spots set to air that evening.

    U.S. District Judge Jennifer L. Rochon granted a temporary restraining order sought by Common Cause, SEIU Local 32BJ and others, finding they are likely to succeed in their claim that the roughly $20 million DHS-funded campaign misused appropriations. The order took effect at 6:45 p.m. EDT, bars the government from paying for or placing more of the ads, and lasts two weeks. The administration had told the court the campaign would end Sunday; it is the first known ruling to block the ads, which also face a DNC lawsuit.

    Why impact 3: A court has now said federal money likely cannot pay for ads praising the president weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms.

    U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New YorkU.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York (order) ↗
  7. Impact 3

    A federal judge struck down the Justice Department's nationwide program for collecting states' unredacted voter rolls, vacating it for the whole country.

    In a 78-page opinion dated Friday, Oct. 9, U.S. District Judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia set aside DOJ's "Voter List Maintenance Policy" in Common Cause v. U.S. Department of Justice, brought by Common Cause and four voters against DOJ and the attorney general. Under the policy, begun in May 2025, the Civil Rights Division requested full voter lists, including names, birth dates, addresses and driver's license or partial Social Security numbers, from 49 states and the District of Columbia, shared the data with Homeland Security to run through the SAVE citizenship database, and urged states to remove voters it deemed ineligible. The court said at least 18 states fully cooperated; others refused, and at least 25 federal district courts have dismissed DOJ suits to force them. The judge ruled that no statute, including the 1960 Civil Rights Act, the National Voter Registration Act or the Help America Vote Act, authorizes DOJ to collect and share unredacted statewide rolls; that DOJ ignored the risk of removing citizens based on records known to be unreliable; and that the policy violated the Privacy Act and the Paperwork Reduction Act. She rejected DOJ's request to limit relief to the plaintiffs, citing the APA's ordinary remedy of vacatur. The ruling does not stop states from maintaining their own rolls.

    Why impact 3: It halts the federal government's nationwide voter-data and citizenship-check program less than four weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, including in states that already handed over their rolls.

    U.S. District Court for the District of ColumbiaU.S. District Court for the District of Columbia (opinion) ↗
  8. Impact 3

    The Democratic National Committee sued the Trump administration over $20 million in taxpayer-funded TV ads it calls illegal pro-Trump propaganda.

    The complaint, filed Wednesday in U.S. District Court in Washington, names President Trump, the White House, Homeland Security and the Office of Management and Budget, and asks the court to declare the ads illegal and stop federal money from paying for them. The ads, marked 'Paid for by the U.S. Government,' began airing in September; $20 million has been drawn from a $175 million DHS immigration-enforcement fund, and more than $12 million had been spent so far, AP reported, citing AdImpact. No ruling yet.

    Why impact 3: A court test of whether federal money can pay for ads praising the president weeks before the midterms.

    U.S.DNC complaint; AP ↗
  9. Impact 3

    A Miami federal judge dismissed a noncitizen-voting case, ruling the federal ban in 18 U.S.C. § 611 is unconstitutional; the Justice Department appealed.

    U.S. District Judge David S. Leibowitz ruled Thursday that Congress cannot set voter qualifications, a power he said belongs to the states, and dismissed the misdemeanor case against Chelsea Cox. CBS News reported the order applies only to Cox and is the first time a court has held the statute unconstitutional. It does not affect the separate law against false claims of citizenship. The Justice Department moved to appeal.

    Why impact 3: A federal criminal voting statute used by the Justice Department failed in court a month before the Nov. 3 midterms, and the government is appealing.

    U.S. District Court, MiamiCBS News ↗
  10. Impact 3

    The Supreme Court declined, for the third time this month, to let Missouri use its 2025 congressional map in this year's elections.

    The 2025 map redrew the 3rd and 5th districts in a way expected to give Republicans one more U.S. House seat. Missouri's Supreme Court ruled on Sept. 3 that a voter referendum petition with over 300,000 signatures must be decided first. The 2022 map will be used in November.

    Why impact 3: Settles which district lines apply for Missouri's eight House seats in the midterms; limited to one state.

    U.S. Supreme CourtSCOTUSblog ↗
  11. Impact 4

    The Supreme Court ruled 7-2 that the administration can use its expanded SAVE database to help states check whether voters are U.S. citizens.

    SAVE is a Homeland Security database originally built to check immigration status; it was expanded to include U.S.-born citizens' records and Social Security numbers. A federal judge had blocked it in June over privacy-law violations. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented. Federal law still bars systematic voter-roll purges within 90 days of an election.

    Why impact 4: Applies nationwide to voter-eligibility checks before the November midterms and involves citizens' personal data; a final ruling on the case is still pending.

    U.S. Supreme CourtSCOTUSblog ↗
