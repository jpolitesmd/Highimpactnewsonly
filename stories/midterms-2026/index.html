  11. Impact 3

    New Hampshire can bar student IDs even as backup proof of identity at the polls on Nov. 3, the Supreme Court ruled Thursday, pausing a judge's order.

    The Court granted Secretary of State David Scanlan's emergency application (Scanlan v. Coalition for Open Democracy, No. 26A512) and paused a federal judge's Oct. 2 and Oct. 5 orders while the case is appealed to the 1st Circuit and, if sought, the Supreme Court. A state law passed this year removed student IDs from the list of accepted photo IDs for getting a ballot; Scanlan's guidance then barred them for any purpose, including under a backup provision that lets officials weigh other evidence of identity. U.S. District Judge Joseph Laplante left the law in place but blocked that guidance. The unsigned order, quoting a 2020 precedent, said lower federal courts "should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election." Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented, writing that the judge's order left the state's rule untouched and only corrected a publicized misstatement of what the law requires. The Republican National Committee backed the state. New Hampshire has a closely contested U.S. Senate race this year.

    Why impact 3: It settles, for this election, which IDs college students in New Hampshire can use to vote, less than two weeks before Election Day.

    U.S. Supreme CourtSupreme Court of the United States (order); AP ↗

Plaintiffs include San Francisco, Santa Clara, the California Nurses Association, National Nurses United and the Voto Latino Foundation. DHS did not immediately comment; Justice Department lawyers had argued that blocking such investigations would harm the public interest.

    Why impact 3: It limits a federal enforcement push in nine states, including several battlegrounds, less than two weeks before the midterms.

  14. Impact 3

    North Carolina's stricter absentee-ballot envelope rules stay in place for the midterms after a federal judge refused Monday to block them.

    U.S. District Judge Thomas D. Schroeder denied a preliminary injunction in North Carolina Alliance for Retired Americans v. North Carolina State Board of Elections. Voting groups and two voters challenged State Board guidance under which a ballot not sealed inside the inner container-return envelope, or found outside it, is spoiled and must be replaced even if the outer envelope is sealed (2024 guidance had accepted a ballot if either envelope was sealed), and a signature-verification step. The judge cited the Supreme Court's Purcell principle against changing election rules once voting is underway: about 123,828 absentee ballots had been requested as of Oct. 8, the State Board said 31,582 had been cast by the Oct. 14 hearing, in-person early voting began Oct. 15, and Election Day is Nov. 3. Voters whose ballots are spoiled can get a new ballot or vote in person. The North Carolina Republican Party and the RNC joined as defendants. The ruling is preliminary, not a final decision on the merits.

    Why impact 3: It sets the mail-ballot rules for a statewide midterm already underway.

  21. Impact 3

    A federal judge struck down the Justice Department's nationwide program for collecting states' unredacted voter rolls, vacating it for the whole country.

    In a 78-page opinion dated Friday, Oct. 9, U.S. District Judge Sparkle L. Sooknanan of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia set aside DOJ's "Voter List Maintenance Policy" in Common Cause v. U.S. Department of Justice, brought by Common Cause and four voters against DOJ and the attorney general. Under the policy, begun in May 2025, the Civil Rights Division requested full voter lists, including names, birth dates, addresses and driver's license or partial Social Security numbers, from 49 states and the District of Columbia, shared the data with Homeland Security to run through the SAVE citizenship database, and urged states to remove voters it deemed ineligible. The court said at least 18 states fully cooperated; others refused, and at least 25 federal district courts have dismissed DOJ suits to force them. The judge ruled that no statute, including the 1960 Civil Rights Act, the National Voter Registration Act or the Help America Vote Act, authorizes DOJ to collect and share unredacted statewide rolls; that DOJ ignored the risk of removing citizens based on records known to be unreliable; and that the policy violated the Privacy Act and the Paperwork Reduction Act. She rejected DOJ's request to limit relief to the plaintiffs, citing the APA's ordinary remedy of vacatur. The ruling does not stop states from maintaining their own rolls.

    Why impact 3: It halts the federal government's nationwide voter-data and citizenship-check program less than four weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, including in states that already handed over their rolls.

    U.S. District Court for the District of ColumbiaU.S. District Court for the District of Columbia (opinion) ↗

  25. Impact 4

    The Supreme Court ruled 7-2 that the administration can use its expanded SAVE database to help states check whether voters are U.S. citizens.

    SAVE is a Homeland Security database originally built to check immigration status; it was expanded to include U.S.-born citizens' records and Social Security numbers. A federal judge had blocked it in June over privacy-law violations. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented. Federal law still bars systematic voter-roll purges within 90 days of an election.

    Why impact 4: Applies nationwide to voter-eligibility checks before the November midterms and involves citizens' personal data; a final ruling on the case is still pending.

    U.S. Supreme CourtSCOTUSblog ↗
