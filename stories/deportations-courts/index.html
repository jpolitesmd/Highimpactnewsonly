Deportation policy in the courts

Court rulings on the administration's deportation policies, including removals to third countries.

12 updates · newest first

  1. Impact 3

    Immigration agents in Southern California may not stop people just for looking Latino or working-class, a federal judge ruled in an order made public Thursday.

    U.S. District Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong's preliminary injunction, issued last week, requires individualized reasonable suspicion before an immigration stop. She cited "a mountain of evidence," including body-camera footage and agents' text messages using slurs for Latinos. The order covers Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, San Bernardino, Ventura, Santa Barbara and San Luis Obispo counties and exempts ports of entry and checkpoints. The Supreme Court stayed her earlier, broader order last year; she said the new request rests on different evidence and legal theories, and noted the ruling is not a final finding of an unlawful policy. The administration filed a notice of appeal to the 9th Circuit on Wednesday. DHS has denied racial profiling.

    Why impact 3: It limits how immigration sweeps can be carried out across the nation's most populous metro region while the case continues.

    U.S. District Court for the Central District of CaliforniaLos Angeles Times ↗
  2. Impact 3

    A Biden-era rule letting immigration judges pause some deportation cases stays thrown out after the Supreme Court declined to restore it Tuesday.

    In a brief unsigned order in City of Baltimore v. Texas (No. 26A500), with no noted dissents, the court refused to pause a June consent judgment between Texas and the Justice Department that vacated the 2024 "administrative closure" rule. Chief Judge Reed O'Connor in Wichita Falls entered that judgment the same day Texas sued, at both parties' request. Baltimore, New Haven, Columbus and Centro Legal de la Raza, which called the deal collusive, were denied permission to join the case; their appeal continues at the 5th Circuit.

    Why impact 3: Immigration judges lose a tool used to set aside cases while people such as crime victims seek relief from a separate agency.

    U.S.Supreme Court order ↗
  3. Impact 3

    Immigrants held by ICE in Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi have no constitutional right to a bond hearing, the full federal appeals court there ruled Friday.

    The 5th Circuit, sitting as a full 17-judge court, ruled 10-7 that people who entered the U.S. without being admitted and are held under 8 U.S.C. 1225(b)(2)(A) are not entitled by the Fifth Amendment's due process clause to hearings on whether they are dangerous or likely to flee, however long their deportation cases take. It reversed lower-court orders that had freed three men, two from Mexico and one from Honduras, who had lived in the U.S. 14 to 22 years and were turned over to ICE after traffic stops, and it overturned a July panel ruling that had required bond hearings within 90 days. Judge Cory Wilson wrote for the majority that detainees can end their detention "at any moment by agreeing to leave the country." Judge Leslie Southwick, writing for six dissenters, said the court had built "an impenetrable wall between the Constitution" and long-term residents; Judge Dana Douglas wrote that unchecked power to detain indefinitely "echoes fearfully from history." Texas and Louisiana hold the largest numbers of immigration detainees in the country. The ruling deepens a split with other appeals courts, and the Supreme Court agreed this month to decide the related question of whether immigration law itself requires bond hearings. DHS general counsel James Percival called it a "big win." The National Immigration Project, which represents the men, said it opens the door to more people being locked up and is weighing whether to appeal.

    Why impact 3: It removes a main path to release for detained immigrants in the states that hold the most of them, while the Supreme Court weighs the issue.

    U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth CircuitU.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit (en banc opinion) ↗
  4. Impact 3

    A challenge to ICE arrests at churches and synagogues was revived Tuesday when a federal appeals court ruled 27 religious groups can seek to block the policy.

    A split D.C. Circuit panel (2-1) reversed U.S. District Judge Dabney Friedrich, who had ruled the Christian and Jewish groups lacked standing for a preliminary injunction, and sent the case back for her to reconsider their request. The groups challenge the Jan. 20, 2025, Homeland Security memo that rescinded the decades-old "sensitive locations" policy limiting immigration enforcement at places of worship. Judge Florence Pan, joined by Judge Robert Wilkins, wrote that the government "announced a plan to ramp up enforcement" and the groups showed a credible threat. Judge Neomi Rao dissented, calling the claimed harms speculative. The ruling does not itself block the policy.

    Why impact 3: It reopens the possibility that limits on immigration arrests at houses of worship could be restored while the case continues.

    U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. CircuitCourthouse News Service (D.C. Circuit opinion) ↗
  5. Impact 3

    The Justice Department's lawsuit against New York City's sanctuary laws was thrown out by a federal judge in Brooklyn on Thursday, leaving the city's limits on helping ICE in place.

    U.S. District Judge Ramón E. Reyes Jr. granted the city's motion to dismiss the suit the administration filed in July 2025. He found the federal government lacked standing to challenge the city's ban on police holding immigrants at federal request because state law already bars that practice, and ruled that federal law does not require local governments to share release dates or other custody information. He rejected the argument that federal law overrides the city's rules, finding the federal government cannot force local governments to assist with immigration enforcement. Mayor Zohran Mamdani called the ruling a victory for New Yorkers.

    Why impact 3: It is a court loss for the administration's campaign against sanctuary cities, and the nation's largest city keeps its rules limiting cooperation with ICE.

    U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New YorkU.S. District Court, E.D.N.Y. (opinion via Courthouse News); Reuters ↗
  6. Impact 3

    California's law requiring plainclothes federal agents to show identification cannot be enforced against them, a federal appeals court ruled.

    A Ninth Circuit panel (Judges Nguyen, Bennett and Collins) on Oct. 14 reversed a district judge and ordered a preliminary injunction barring California, Gov. Gavin Newsom and Attorney General Rob Bonta from enforcing Section 10 of the No Vigilantes Act against federal agencies and officers. That section requires non-uniformed federal officers working in California to visibly display ID, with criminal penalties for violators. The court said the Justice Department is likely to win its claim that the state cannot directly regulate how federal officers do their jobs under the Constitution's Supremacy Clause.

    Why impact 3: It lets federal immigration and other agents keep working in California without displaying ID while the case continues.

    U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth CircuitU.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit (opinion) ↗
  7. Impact 3

    The State Department must open the 2027 diversity visa lottery, which it had skipped this year, a federal judge ordered.

    U.S. District Judge Jinsook Ohta in San Diego granted a preliminary injunction Oct. 14 in Red Eagle Law v. Rubio. She ordered Secretary of State Marco Rubio to publicly announce the DV-2027 registration period, including dates, the entry website and requirements, by midnight Oct. 15, including in the Federal Register, and to keep registration open for 30 days. The department must accept eligible electronic entries, notify those selected and process applications until the visas run out or the fiscal year ends Sept. 30, 2027. The program offers up to 55,000 green cards a year; the administration had not opened registration on time and argued it was too late.

    Why impact 3: Up to 55,000 green cards for 2027 are back in play after the program had effectively been canceled.

    U.S. District Court for the Southern District of CaliforniaU.S. District Court for the Southern District of California (order, via Courthouse News) ↗
  8. Impact 3

    The Trump administration's rule for fining migrants up to $998 a day for not leaving the U.S. was made final today, after a Boston judge stayed the earlier version.

    Homeland Security and the Justice Department finalized the June 2025 interim rule with one change: ICE may accept a late appeal when a documented mail delay was beyond the person's control. Appeals are still due within 15 business days. The rule covers fines for failing to leave after a final removal order (up to $998 a day), missing a voluntary-departure deadline and being caught entering illegally. A federal judge in Massachusetts stayed the interim rule and the fines issued under it, finding ICE likely skipped required public comment; the departments say that stay does not stop the final rule from taking effect immediately. DHS also proposed today to end the rule telling USCIS officers to defer to earlier approvals when deciding H-1B, L-1, O-1 and other work-visa extensions; comments are due Nov. 13.

    Why impact 3: The administration is trying to restart more than 100,000 fines worth about $84 billion that a court had halted.

    U.S.Federal Register (DHS/DOJ final rule) ↗
  9. Impact 2

    An ICE officer shot a man in his car with a 5-year-old in the back seat in Manhattan, and Mayor Zohran Mamdani demanded ICE halt city operations.

    The Dominican man, identified as Oscar Belgal, was wounded in the neck during an arrest Oct. 8 in Marble Hill and is held outside New York state. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said officers did not know the child was in the car and criticized the city's sanctuary policies.

    Why impact 2: A sharp new clash between New York City and federal immigration agents.

    U.S.AP ↗
  10. Impact 3

    A federal judge in Boston blocked the Trump administration's policy of fining migrants up to $1.8 million for not leaving the U.S. after final deportation orders.

    U.S. District Judge George O'Toole stayed the ICE rule used to impose the fines and barred enforcement of fines issued after June 27, 2025, while a class-action lawsuit brought by two fined migrants moves forward, Reuters reported Monday. He found the fines were likely imposed unlawfully, including because ICE skipped public notice and comment and the Department of Homeland Security did not consider individual circumstances. Since last year the administration has levied fines of $998 a day, applied retroactively for up to five years. DHS said in July it had issued more than 100,000 such fines totaling about $84 billion.

    Why impact 3: The order halts collection of more than 100,000 fines worth about $84 billion while the case proceeds.

    U.S.Reuters ↗
  11. Impact 3

    The Supreme Court let the administration resume deporting immigrants to countries other than their own while the case continues.

    Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson would have denied the request. The order pauses a Massachusetts federal judge's ruling, upheld by the 1st Circuit, that the policy denied immigrants a chance to raise fears of torture or persecution in the destination country. Deportations have included flights to El Salvador, Libya and South Sudan. The court will hear arguments in December, with a decision expected by summer 2027.

    Why impact 3: Deportations can resume now, but the order is temporary: the court hears the case in December and rules by summer 2027.

    U.S. Supreme CourtCBS News ↗
  12. Impact 3

    Federal Judge Stephanie Gallagher held the administration in contempt for twice deporting an asylum seeker to El Salvador in violation of court orders.

    The man, identified as E.L.A., was protected by orders from a 2019 class-action case covering immigrants who arrived as unaccompanied minors. He returned to the U.S. on Aug. 7 after the first removal. The judge ordered the government to bring him back and report by Oct. 2. Court filings say at least 107 protected people were removed before their asylum cases were decided.

    Why impact 3: A court finding that the executive branch defied court orders; it affects one case directly but bears on at least 107 others.

    U.S. District Court, MarylandABC News ↗
