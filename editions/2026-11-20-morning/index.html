  1. Impact 3

    A sweeping bill to speed up federal permits for power lines, pipelines, mines and wind and solar farms passed the Senate Thursday night and now goes to the House.

    The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (H.R. 2347 as amended) passed 62-33. Fifteen members of the Democratic caucus voted yes along with nearly all Republicans; 31 Democrats and Republican Sens. Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds voted no, many Democrats citing weaker environmental protections. The bill shortens reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act, Endangered Species Act and Clean Water Act, gives federal regulators new authority over interstate transmission lines, and aims to keep power customers from paying for grid hookups to data centers. President Trump backs the deal; the House is expected to take it up after Thanksgiving.

    Why impact 3: It is the biggest change in years to how energy and infrastructure projects get approved, with backers saying it will lower power bills.

  2. Impact 3

    Stock markets fell around the world Friday as the AI sell-off deepened, putting Asian shares on track for their worst week since March 2020.

    Japan's Nikkei fell 5%, and Taiwan's market was down 17% for the week, its biggest weekly drop since 1990. MSCI's index of Asia-Pacific shares outside Japan is down 8% this week. Europe's STOXX 600 fell 1.5% and Nasdaq 100 futures fell 1.5%; MSCI's world index fell for a seventh straight session, its longest losing streak since 2023. Investors bought bonds, pushing the 10-year Treasury yield below 5%. Anthropic's record IPO Thursday did not restore confidence in AI spending, analysts told Reuters.

    Why impact 3: A global slide in AI stocks hits retirement accounts and could weigh on the economy.

  3. Impact 3

    The White House national cyber director, Sean Cairncross, was fired after a week of outages hit air travel, banks and the NSA, a person close to the White House said.

    The source said the president "fully supported" the firing; Defense Department chief information officer Kirsten Davies takes the job temporarily. Cairncross, confirmed in August 2025, said it was the "honor of a lifetime" to serve. The top U.S. intelligence official said Wednesday the outages were separate maintenance and technical failures, not a coordinated or state-sponsored attack. A permanent successor needs Senate confirmation; Democrats take control of the Senate in January.

    Why impact 3: It is the administration's first personnel move after outages that grounded flights and froze bank accounts.

    U.S.Reuters ↗
  4. Impact 2

    Florida executed Thomas Overton for the 1991 murders of a Florida Keys couple, the state's 20th execution this year, a record.

    Overton, 71, was pronounced dead at 6:19 p.m. at Florida State Prison after the U.S. Supreme Court denied a stay; his last words were "I never killed anyone." He was convicted of raping and killing Missy MacIvor, who was eight months pregnant, and killing her husband, Michael. Florida executed 19 people in 2025; two more executions are scheduled in December. Later Thursday, Texas executed Reinaldo Dennes, 70, in a procedure that took over an hour as staff struggled to find a vein, AP reported.

    Why impact 2: Florida now carries out more executions than the rest of the country combined.

    U.S.Palm Beach Post (USA TODAY Network) ↗
  5. Impact 2

    Baseball's career hits leader Pete Rose was posthumously pardoned by President Trump for his 1990 tax crimes.

    Trump announced the "Complete and Total Pardon" on social media Thursday. Rose, who died in 2024 with 4,256 hits, pleaded guilty in 1990 to filing false tax returns, months after accepting a lifetime ban from baseball over betting. Major League Baseball reinstated him last year.

    Why impact 2: It clears the record of one of baseball's most famous and divisive figures.

    U.S.Reuters ↗
  6. Impact 4

    Anthropic's stock closed its first trading day about 17% above its $83 IPO price, but the wider market fell about 2% as AI shares slid.

    Anthropic opened at $111.50 shortly before noon, briefly valuing the company above $2 trillion and topping $115, before fading to close near $97. The S&P 500 erased an early 1.3% gain and fell about 2% for a sixth straight loss, its longest losing streak since April 2024; the Dow fell about 1,100 points (2.2%) to back below 50,000 and the Nasdaq lost about 2.3%. AMD dropped about 10% after investigative outlet Hunterbrook disclosed a short position citing its China dealings; AMD denied wrongdoing.

    Why impact 4: The record IPO was seen as a test of investor faith in AI, and it did not stop a weeks-long slide in tech stocks.

  7. Impact 4

    Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the U.N.'s top court ruled Thursday, the first time it has found a country itself responsible for genocide.

    The International Court of Justice found that Myanmar's security forces intentionally killed Rohingya, caused them serious bodily or mental harm and imposed measures meant to prevent births during the 2017 "clearance operations" in northern Rakhine state, and that Myanmar failed to prevent and punish genocide. The court cited an estimated 10,000 Rohingya killed; about 700,000 fled to Bangladesh. It ordered Myanmar to investigate and prosecute those responsible, allow safe return of displaced Rohingya, and compensate them, with the amount to be set later; Myanmar must report back within two years. The judgment in the case brought by The Gambia in 2019 is final, binding and without appeal. U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres welcomed it.

    Why impact 4: It is the first time the world court has held a state directly responsible for committing genocide.

    WorldUN News ↗
  8. Impact 3

    A resolution directing President Trump to pull U.S. forces out of the Iran war failed in the Senate, 48-51, in Congress's first war vote since the midterms.

    Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Rand Paul voted for the House-passed measure (H.Con.Res. 93); Democrat John Fetterman voted against it, and Republican Thom Tillis, who backed an earlier attempt, also voted no. As a concurrent resolution it would not have gone to the president. Democrats say they will keep pushing war-powers votes and use spending bills to end the war once they control Congress in January. The Pentagon lists 23 U.S. service members killed in the conflict.

    Why impact 3: It shows Senate Republicans still backing the war weeks after voters punished the party over it.

    U.S.Reuters ↗
  9. Impact 3

    Russia is "comfortable" with NATO-like security guarantees for Ukraine after the war, U.S. envoy Jared Kushner said after talks with European leaders in Berlin.

    Kushner and Steve Witkoff met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and dozens of European leaders; Trump called into the meeting after his talks with Xi and Putin in China. Kushner said an Article 5-style U.S. guarantee is "a really, really big deal" that Trump is open to if it serves U.S. interests, and that Russia agreed to talks that include Europe. No date is set for the next round. Putin, speaking in Shenzhen, vowed to respond to Ukraine's strikes on the Kerch Bridge and said he would not be negotiating ceasefires.

    Why impact 3: Security guarantees are a key sticking point in any deal to end the nearly five-year war.

    WorldAP ↗
  10. Impact 3

    The Comanchero motorcycle gang and Italy's 'Ndrangheta mafia were sanctioned by the Treasury Department Thursday for helping drug cartels move cocaine and launder money.

    OFAC said the Australia-based Comancheros worked with the Sinaloa Cartel, CJNG and other cartels on multi-hundred-kilogram cocaine shipments, and named 'Ndrangheta leaders and funeral-home and limousine businesses in Ontario, Canada. The action was coordinated with police in Australia, New Zealand, Italy and Canada. In a separate action the same day, Treasury sanctioned cyber-scam operators in Cambodia and facilitators tied to an armed group in Myanmar's Karen State.

    Why impact 3: It extends the administration's anti-cartel sanctions to the global gangs that supply and finance them.

  11. Impact 3

    Heidi Overton's nomination to lead the Food and Drug Administration was withdrawn by President Trump the night before a key Senate committee vote, citing too little support.

    A White House official said the nomination lacked enough support in Congress; the Senate health committee, where Republicans hold a one-seat edge, was due to vote Wednesday. Committee chair Bill Cassidy said the White House "abandoned a viable confirmation path." Overton, a physician and White House domestic policy aide, faced criticism over her views on vaccines and abortion. Sen. Roger Marshall of Kansas is being weighed as a replacement, sources told Reuters. The FDA has had no Senate-confirmed leader since Marty Makary left in May. (Missed in our Nov. 18-19 editions.)

    Why impact 3: The agency that approves drugs and oversees food safety will likely go without a confirmed chief into next year.

    U.S.Reuters ↗
  12. Impact 3

    A Homeland Security rule capping how long foreign students, exchange visitors and foreign journalists can stay in the U.S. was struck down by a federal judge in Boston Monday.

    U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor vacated the July rule nationwide, finding it "arbitrary and capricious" and beyond DHS's authority. It would have replaced open-ended "duration of status" admission for F, J and I visa holders with fixed stays of up to four years, required many to apply for extensions, and limited some PhD, joint-degree and program changes. "DHS is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity... that Congress sought to permit and encourage," Saylor wrote. The rule never took effect after he blocked it in September; the government's appeal of that order is pending. (Missed in our Nov. 16-17 editions.)

    Why impact 3: It keeps the decades-old rules for more than a million international students and visiting journalists.
